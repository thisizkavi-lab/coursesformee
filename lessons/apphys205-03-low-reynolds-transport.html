Chapter 03: Life at Low Reynolds Number & Diffusion Limits

1. The Reynolds Number in Microbiology

The Navier-Stokes equations dictate fluid dynamics. The relative importance of inertial forces versus viscous forces is captured by a dimensionless parameter known as the Reynolds number ($Re$):

$$ Re = \frac{\rho v L}{\eta} $$

For a human swimming in a pool, $Re \approx 10^4$ (inertia dominates). For an E. coli bacterium ($L \approx 1 \text{ } \mu\text{m}$, $v \approx 20 \text{ } \mu\text{m/s}$) in water ($\eta \approx 10^{-3} \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$), $Re \approx 10^{-4}$. At these low Reynolds numbers, inertia is entirely negligible. If a swimming bacterium stops moving its flagella, it will coast to a halt in less than the diameter of an atom!

2. Purcell's Scallop Theorem

Because the Stokes equation (Navier-Stokes without the inertial terms) is time-reversible, any reciprocal motion (like a scallop opening and closing its shell) results in zero net displacement. To swim at low Reynolds number, an organism must employ non-reciprocal motion. E. coli achieves this by rotating a helical flagellum.

Non-Reciprocal Motion (Helical Propeller)
      
       (Cell Body)
         /-----\      Rotation
        |       | --->   ~~~~===~~~~===~~~~=== (Helical Flagellum)
         \-----/

    * Does not look the same played forwards and backwards in time.
    * Breaks time-reversibility -> Net thrust generated.

3. Brownian Motion & The Berg-Purcell Limit

At the microscale, molecules move primarily by diffusion. The Stokes-Einstein relation connects the diffusion constant $D$ to temperature and fluid viscosity:

$$ D = \frac{k_B T}{6 \pi \eta a} $$

How well can a cell measure its environment? The Berg-Purcell limit describes the fundamental uncertainty in concentration measurement due to the random arrival of molecules at a receptor. The fractional error in estimating concentration $c$ over a time $\tau$ by a receptor of size $a$ is:

$$ \frac{\delta c}{c} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{D a c \tau}} $$

4. Simulating Berg-Purcell Sensory Noise

Let's write a short python script to compute the Berg-Purcell noise limit for a typical bacterial sensing task.

import numpy as np

def berg_purcell_error(c_uM, a_nm, tau_s, T_K=300, eta=1e-3):
    """
    Computes the fractional error (noise-to-signal ratio) of concentration sensing.
    """
    # Constants
    k_B = 1.38e-23
    N_A = 6.022e23
    
    # Unit conversions
    a_m = a_nm * 1e-9
    c_m3 = c_uM * 1e-6 * 1e3 * N_A  # molecules / m^3
    
    # Stokes-Einstein Diffusion (assume small molecule radius ~ 1nm)
    r_molecule = 1e-9
    D = (k_B * T_K) / (6 * np.pi * eta * r_molecule)
    
    # Berg-Purcell equation
    variance = 1 / (D * a_m * c_m3 * tau_s)
    fractional_error = np.sqrt(variance)
    
    return fractional_error

error = berg_purcell_error(c_uM=1.0, a_nm=10.0, tau_s=1.0)
print(f"Fractional Error for 1uM sensing over 1s: {error*100:.2f}%")

Exercise: Coasting to a stop

Calculate analytically the coasting distance of a 1 micron bacterium swimming at 20 microns/sec if the driving force instantly drops to zero. Assume spherical geometry for simplicity.

Reveal Step-by-Step Solution

Using $m \frac{dv}{dt} = -6\pi\eta r v$, the velocity decays exponentially with time constant $\tau = \frac{m}{6\pi\eta r}$. Integrating velocity to get distance gives $x_{coast} = v_0 \tau$. For E. coli, this distance is on the order of 0.1 Angstroms!
