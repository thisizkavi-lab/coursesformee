Chapter 05: Molecular Motors & Non-Equilibrium Ratchet Mechanisms

1. Kinesin and Molecular Walking

Molecular motors like Kinesin and Myosin convert chemical energy (ATP hydrolysis, $\Delta G_{\text{ATP}} \approx 20-25 \text{ } k_B T$) into mechanical work. Kinesin "walks" along microtubules taking distinct 8 nm steps. It can produce forces up to roughly 6-7 pN before stalling.

2. The Flashing Ratchet Model

At the nanoscale, thermal noise is violent. How do you move forward in a storm? You rectify the noise. The Feynman-Smoluchowski ratchet demonstrates that you cannot extract work from equilibrium thermal fluctuations. However, by coupling the system to a non-equilibrium chemical reaction (ATP hydrolysis), the potential energy landscape can "flash" between an asymmetric saw-tooth potential and a flat potential.

State 1: Asymmetric Potential (ATP bound)
      /\          /\
     /  \        /  \
    /    \      /    \
___/      \____/      \___

State 2: Flat Potential (ATP hydrolyzed, free diffusion)
__________________________

* When potential is flat, particle diffuses symmetrically.
* When potential turns on, particle slides down the closest steep slope.
* Net result: Directed motion to the right without a macroscopic "push"!
Crucial Insight: Motors do not "swim" or "push" their way through the fluid like macroscopic engines. They bias the probability of thermal fluctuations. The energy of ATP is spent to prevent backward steps, rectifying Brownian motion.

3. Simulating a 1D Langevin Flashing Ratchet

Let's simulate a particle undergoing Brownian motion, where the underlying potential toggles on and off.

import numpy as np

def simulate_flashing_ratchet(steps=10000, dt=0.01, flash_period=50):
    """
    Simulates a 1D flashing ratchet.
    """
    x = 0.0
    trajectory = []
    
    for step in range(steps):
        # Brownian noise step
        dx_noise = np.random.normal(0, np.sqrt(2 * dt))
        
        # Determine if potential is ON or OFF
        if (step // flash_period) % 2 == 0:
            # ON state: Asymmetric force
            # Simple saw-tooth gradient approximation
            phase = x % 1.0
            if phase < 0.8:
                force = -1.0  # Slow climb to the right
            else:
                force = 4.0   # Fast drop to the right
        else:
            # OFF state: Flat potential
            force = 0.0
            
        dx_drift = force * dt
        x += dx_drift + dx_noise
        trajectory.append(x)
        
    return trajectory

traj = simulate_flashing_ratchet()
print(f"Final particle position: {traj[-1]:.2f} (Net directed motion!)")
