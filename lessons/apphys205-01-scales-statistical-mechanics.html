Chapter 01: Biological Scales & Statistical Mechanics of the Cell

1. Length, Time, and Energy Scales

Before writing equations, we must orient ourselves in the universe of the cell. Biology happens at scales spanning 1 nm (the diameter of a DNA helix) to 10 μm (a typical mammalian cell). An E. coli bacterium is roughly a cylinder 1 μm in diameter and 2 μm long, possessing a volume of roughly 1 femtoliter ($10^{-15}$ L).

At these scales, the dominant energy currency is thermal energy. The characteristic scale is defined by the product of Boltzmann's constant $k_B$ and the absolute temperature $T$. At room temperature (roughly 300 K), $k_B T \approx 4.1 \text{ pN} \cdot \text{nm}$.

Rule of Thumb: Always compare the energy of a biological interaction to $k_B T$. If a process involves energies much less than $k_B T$, it will be completely washed out by thermal noise. ATP hydrolysis releases about $20-25 \text{ } k_B T$, safely above the thermal noise floor, allowing directed work.

2. Statistical Mechanics & The Boltzmann Distribution

Statistical mechanics connects the microscopic interactions of molecules to macroscopic observables. The cornerstone of equilibrium statistical mechanics is the Boltzmann distribution, which states that the probability $P_i$ of a system being in a specific microstate $i$ with energy $E_i$ is:

$$P_i = \frac{1}{Z} e^{-E_i / k_B T}$$

Where $Z = \sum_j e^{-E_j / k_B T}$ is the partition function, representing the sum over all possible microstates.

Free Energy Minimization ($F = E - TS$)

We often care about macrostates—collections of microstates that look identical macroscopically. The probability of a macrostate depends on both its energy and its multiplicity (entropy, $S$). This gives rise to the Helmholtz Free Energy $F = E - TS$. Biological systems at equilibrium naturally minimize their free energy.

3. Computing Equilibrium Probabilities

Let's simulate a two-state system, such as an ion channel gating or protein unfolding, varying the energy difference and observing the thermal equilibrium probabilities.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def two_state_probability(delta_E_kBT):
    """
    Computes the probability of being in state 2 given the energy difference
    delta_E = E2 - E1 in units of k_B T.
    """
    # Partition function relative to state 1 (E1 = 0)
    Z = 1 + np.exp(-delta_E_kBT)
    P2 = np.exp(-delta_E_kBT) / Z
    return P2

energies = np.linspace(-5, 5, 100)
probabilities = two_state_probability(energies)

print(f"P(State 2) at ΔE = 0 kBT: {two_state_probability(0):.2f}")
print(f"P(State 2) at ΔE = 3 kBT: {two_state_probability(3):.4f}")
print(f"P(State 2) at ΔE = -3 kBT: {two_state_probability(-3):.4f}")

Exercise: Ion Channel Voltage Gating

Assume the energy difference between the open and closed states of an ion channel depends linearly on the transmembrane voltage $V$: $\Delta E(V) = q V + \Delta E_0$. Write a function to calculate the open probability as a function of voltage.

Reveal Step-by-Step Solution
python

def p_open(V, q, dE0, T=300):
    k_B = 1.38e-23  # J/K
    k_BT = k_B * T
    # Convert everything to Joules
    delta_E = q * V + dE0
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(delta_E / k_BT))
